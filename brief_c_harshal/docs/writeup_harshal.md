## 3. Which approaches did you consider, which did you choose, and why?

I evaluated several methodological options across key stages:
- **Identifier Matching**: I considered fuzzy matching on dates and amounts, exact string matching, and regex suffix normalization. I chose regex normalization to strip non-alphanumeric prefixes and match on numeric tokens. This resolved the critical schema shift between Q1 (`INV-2026-XXXXXX`) and Q2 (`INV2026XXXXXX`), lifting match rates above 96% while avoiding false positive pairings of same-day, same-amount visits.
- **Defining "Collected" Revenue**: I considered counting all payment rows, counting net settlements after fees, and counting only successful gateway captures. I chose `status == 'captured'`, isolating failed and refunded transactions into dedicated audit tables to reflect true realized cash without confounding gateway fee structures.
- **Bridge Architecture**: I considered a simple two-bar comparison (Invoiced vs. Collected) versus a multi-step waterfall bridge. I chose the waterfall bridge because a binary comparison conceals whether missing money is uncollected, untracked, or in transit.

## 5. What broke, what did you try, and what did you change?

Three major technical issues arose during development:
1. **Currency Unit Mismatch**: Gateway payment amounts were denominated in paise, whereas invoices were in rupees. An initial naive join suggested an absurd deficit of over Rs 12 Crores. I audited the raw API schema, confirmed the 100x multiplier, and divided payment values by 100 upon ingestion.
2. **Join Fan-Out & Revenue Inflation**: Performing a `LEFT JOIN` from invoices to payments produced more output rows than input invoices, causing calculated collections to artificially exceed invoiced revenue. Printing duplicate keys revealed that patients who experienced payment retries or split payments had multiple `pay_` IDs attached to a single invoice reference. I resolved this by grouping and aggregating payment amounts by normalized invoice ID *before* joining.
3. **Thousand-Separator String Ingestion**: Invoice amount columns were loaded as object types due to embedded commas (e.g., `"1,499.00"`). Coercing numeric conversion initially produced silent `NaN` values. I reloaded the data specifying `thousands=','` in pandas to ensure numeric integrity from step zero.

## 6. What do you understand about a diagnostics business now that you did not before?

In retail e-commerce, order placement and payment capture occur simultaneously in a single transaction. In diagnostics, clinical operations, billing, and cash collection are fundamentally asynchronous. A patient may book online, have a sample drawn at home or in a clinic, receive an automated invoice, and pay via UPI on delivery, cash at a clinic reception desk, or delayed corporate billing. 

Because collection channels are fragmented across physical clinic cash drawers and third-party digital gateways, financial leakage often represents an integration or logging gap rather than an outright default. Without strict reconciliation between laboratory results and invoice records, a diagnostic provider risks performing expensive tests without capturing the corresponding revenue.

## 8. Which Week 1 or 2 idea do you understand better after using it on this data?

The concept of the **Waterfall Bridge** and **Finite State Entity Reconciliation** became crystal clear through this exercise. In classroom lectures, a waterfall bridge looks like a simple presentation chart. On raw company data, building a mathematically watertight bridge requires strict conservation of rows and rupees: every row must be partitioned into mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive categories (matched, timing, true leak, unallocated). If input does not equal kept plus rejected, the entire analysis fails audit.
