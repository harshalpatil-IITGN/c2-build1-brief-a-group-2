Q3. Which approaches did you consider for your part, which did you choose, why not the others?
Answer :For my part, I considered different approaches to defining test volume, handling missing data, cleaning records, and comparing the actual performance against the business plan.First, for calculating test volume, I considered using either the bookings table or the results table. I chose the results table because each row represents a test performed, whereas bookings can include tests that were never performed, cancellations, and rescheduled bookings. This made the results table a more appropriate starting point for measuring completed testing activity.
Second, for NULL test codes, I considered dropping the records, assigning them as UNKNOWN, or recovering the codes from the bookings table. I chose to recover a code only when there was exactly one corresponding unmatched booked test. This avoided inventing information or introducing duplicate matches. I recovered 2,108 test codes and retained the remaining 94 records in the overall volume, labelling their codes as UNKNOWN.
Third, I considered different ways of handling joins, date formats, and city names. I cleaned identifiers consistently, used a LEFT JOIN to retain results without matching bookings, parsed the different timestamp formats separately, and standardized Bangalore and Bengaluru as the same city.
Finally, I considered different ways to decompose test-volume growth. I used a driver tree based on patients, visits per patient, tests per visit, and results per booked test, then examined the contribution of cities and channels. I chose this approach because the branches reconcile to the overall total, making it easier to identify the drivers of growth and the source of any discrepancy.
The main principle behind my choices was to preserve valid records, avoid double-counting, and make every assumption explicit. The package-counting question remains unresolved because we do not have a package-composition table to establish how many individual tests each package contains.

Answer: The number I trust most is the **5.75% year-on-year growth in test volume**, calculated using the results table for the first half of 2026 compared with the corresponding period in 2025.
The number is based on 357,769 result rows in H1 2025 and 378,358 result rows in H1 2026.
The calculation is:
Growth = ((378,358 − 357,769) / 357,769) × 100
This gives approximately 5.75% growth.
The denominator is the H1 2025 result-row count of 357,769. I trust this measure because the results table represents tests performed rather than tests merely booked, and the analysis retained genuine results even when their test codes or corresponding bookings were missing.
However, I would not consider this number completely definitive. I would stop trusting it as a measure of true individual diagnostic test volume if I discovered that result rows did not consistently represent individual tests, that records were duplicated, or that package bookings represented multiple constituent tests that were not separately counted.

Therefore, I trust the 5.75% figure as a result-row-based growth measure, but I would qualify it as the final business test-volume measure until the package definition and plan denominator are confirmed.

Q5. What broke, what did you try, and what did you change?
Answer: The biggest challenge was that the data was not consistent across years and systems. The 2026 bookings from Jaipur and Chennai had a different structure, with test codes stored as NULL and the tests and patient information embedded in nested fields. This initially made it difficult to match bookings with results and understand the number of tests booked.
I first examined the data structure and compared row counts, missing values, and identifiers across the tables. I then expanded the nested test lists into individual rows and separated the patient information into a consistent structure. This reduced unresolved NULL test codes from approximately 154 to 8 in the affected booking data.
Another problem was that the timestamps used different formats. Applying one automatic date-parsing rule incorrectly interpreted approximately 78,000 records as falling between July and December. I changed the approach to parse ISO timestamps and day-first date formats separately, then checked the parsed dates against the booking IDs. This resolved the date-parsing issue.
I also encountered mismatches between booking IDs and result records, as well as results without matching bookings. Instead of dropping these records or forcing a match, I retained genuine result rows, assigned city using the booking ID prefix where justified, and left the channel unknown when it could not be established.
Finally, I investigated why the number of results per booked test appeared lower in Jaipur and Chennai. I initially considered whether the software change was causing missing results. However, after removing 1,500 rescheduled bookings that had new booking IDs but no results, the ratio became approximately 0.82 to 0.83 across all cities. The evidence also showed that the patient shortfall existed earlier in the year, so I did not attribute it solely to the software change.
These experiences taught me to profile data before joining tables, validate every transformation against row counts and business logic, and distinguish a confirmed data-quality issue from a hypothesis about its cause.

Q6.  What do you understand about a diagnostics business now that you did not before?
Answer: Understanding Operational Metrics and Payment Lifecycles in a Diagnostic Business
One of my key learnings from analyzing a diagnostic business is that different operational metrics can provide different perspectives on business performance. In particular, patient volume, sample volume, and test volume should be treated as distinct metrics rather than used interchangeably.
Patient volume measures the number of unique patients served, sample volume measures the number of specimens collected, and test volume measures the number of individual diagnostic tests performed. Since a single patient may provide multiple samples and a single sample may be used for multiple tests, these metrics can grow at different rates. Analyzing the relationships between them, such as tests per patient and tests per sample, helps explain whether growth is being driven by patient acquisition, testing intensity, or changes in the diagnostic test mix.
Another important learning concerns payment processing. Creating an order or invoice does not establish that a payment has been completed. Similarly, payment authorization indicates approval by the issuing bank but does not necessarily mean that the merchant has collected the funds. Payment capture indicates that the authorized transaction has been submitted for collection, whereas settlement and merchant bank credit represent subsequent stages in the movement of funds.
Therefore, the appropriate definition of a successful payment depends on the business metric being measured. For example, a payment capture rate should be based on successfully captured payments, while a settled payment value or cash-realization metric should be based on settlement or confirmed bank-credit information.
The broader lesson is that business metrics must be defined according to the underlying business event, unit of measurement, population, and time period. Distinguishing between operational activity, transaction status, and realized business outcomes helps prevent misleading conclusions and enables more accurate business performance analysis.
